Public Rooms (Lobby)

The lobby is a public board where hosts advertise the game they are waiting on, so you can find someone to play with instead of swapping addresses by hand. Everything NetPlay does still works exactly as before — the board only automates the address exchange.

It is opt-in: the first time you use it you are asked to agree, and it can be switched off entirely under Options->Settings->NetPlay->Public rooms.

Publishing your game

Tap Start Game as usual. If the board is enabled your room appears on it automatically, and the waiting screen tells you so along with your room code — eight characters such as K7M2QP4A. The alphabet has no I, L, O or U, so it can be read aloud over the phone without confusion.

The board never shows an address. It shows the game, the country, the mode, the input delay, how long the host has been waiting, and a colour telling you how likely the connection is to work.

Joining a game

Tap Public Rooms, pick a room and you are connected. Rooms you cannot use are still listed, greyed out, rather than hidden: knowing a game exists is useful even when this build has no such driver.

Before anything is sent, MAME4droid checks you actually have the ROM, looking in your ROMs folder for name.zip, name.7z or a folder called name. If it is missing you are told which file you need, and the room is left untouched for someone who can play it.

What the colours mean

  • Green — good connection expected. Neither side has a router to get through: you are on the same network, or both of you have working IPv6, or neither router rewrites ports unpredictably.
  • Amber — may not connect. One side is behind a router that changes its public port for every destination (Symmetric NAT, very common on mobile data). It often works, and trying costs nothing.
  • Red — cannot connect. Both sides are behind that kind of router with no way through. The room is shown and the join refused: a stated reason beats a minute of silence.

The colour is a forecast, not a promise. It is worked out from what each side measured about its own router the last time it played, so a brand-new install shows no opinion about itself until it has hosted or joined once.

Private rooms

Set a PIN under Options->Settings->NetPlay and tick Needs a PIN when you create the game. The room still appears on the board, marked as protected, but only someone who knows the PIN can take it. The PIN itself never reaches the server, only a hash of it, and a handful of wrong guesses closes the room to joins.

Drop-in: play now, be joined later

With Rollback selected, the game options offer Start playing now, join whenever. Your game starts immediately and stays on the board while you play. Whoever joins is lifted into the game already in progress: your machine's state travels across to them, so the picture stops for a moment as it does.

  • If they leave, you carry on playing on your own, exactly as if NetPlay had never been involved. Open NetPlay again to put the room back up.
  • Rollback only. The state transfer that makes drop-in possible does not exist in Lockstep.
  • Starting a drop-in while you are already playing may restart the game. See below for when, and why.

Why a drop-in sometimes restarts the game

NetPlay needs both machines to behave identically, frame by frame. Several of the things that decide that are settled the moment a game boots: whether your saved settings and NVRAM are loaded, whether Lua plugins and cheats run, the sound rate, and the working copy of the hard disk in CHD games such as Killer Instinct. NetPlay fixes all of them as it launches the game, and none of them can be changed in a game already running.

That is the whole rule. A drop-in started from the ROM list launches the game with everything fixed. A drop-in started while you are already playing keeps that game only if NetPlay was what launched it — which includes carrying on alone after a previous session ended, and opening the door again. Any other game has to be restarted, and you are told so before you commit.

Ignoring this would not fail loudly: the two machines would drift apart over the following minutes with no error to point at. That is why it restarts rather than trying its luck.

When it doesn't work

  • Two devices on the same Wi-Fi that cannot see each other. Modern routers offer several bands under one network name, and some do not pass traffic between them. If a LAN game refuses to connect, put both devices on the same band, or check that the router is not isolating its clients.
  • Several players on one connection. The server counts by public address, not by device, so everyone in the same house shares one allowance of rooms and requests. It is sized for a roomful of people, but if the board says it is busy, that is what happened — it clears on its own.
  • Mobile data. Two players both on 4G/5G often cannot reach each other at all: carriers frequently isolate their subscribers. IPv6 sidesteps the whole problem when both sides have it, which is why Auto is worth trying under Network protocol.
  • "Waking the board up". The server sleeps when nobody has used it for a while and takes a few seconds to come back. That is a wait, not a failure. If it says the board is not available, it really is off.
  • Both of you must be running the same game. The board tells you which one a room is playing; load it before joining.

Privacy, and why the board works this way

MAME4droid is made by one person and given away. There is no advertising, nothing to unlock, nothing to subscribe to, and there never will be — which also means there is no budget for servers. The board is a good compromise, and what makes finding a game possible at all: a tiny free instance with no database, storing nothing about you, forgetting a room a minute after its host stops asking to keep it alive.

Your game never travels through it. The board introduces two players to each other and then gets out of the way: from that moment the two devices talk directly, and the server has no idea whether you ever connected. That is also why there is no account and no login — keeping personal data would mean protecting it, and the safest data is the data nobody has.

There is no chat, and there will not be one. A chat inside the app would mean moderating what strangers say to each other, which is a real responsibility and a set of store obligations that a project this size cannot honestly meet. Use something built for it — Discord, a group chat, whatever you already share with your friends. The room code is the only thing you need to pass along.

What is sent, and what is kept

To introduce you to another player the board needs your public address and the port NetPlay uses — there is no way around that, it is what the other device dials. Along with it go the game you are playing, the country your SIM or your language says you are in, and, when a session ends, how it went and how fast the connection was.

What matters is what happens to it afterwards:

  • Your address is never written down. It lives in memory while your room is up, is handed only to the player who claims it, and disappears with the room. It appears in no log file, ever. The session reports the server keeps contain no address at all.
  • The board never shows an address to anyone else. When a room says it is on your network, that is a comparison of two salted fingerprints, not a disclosure of either side.
  • Nothing identifies your device. No account, no login, no name, no advertising id, nothing installed on your phone that another session could recognise. Two visits from you are not connected to each other.
  • Nothing survives a restart. Rooms live in memory for a minute at a time, and the salt that makes the fingerprints is new every time the server starts — so yesterday's records could not be matched against today's even by the server itself.
  • You choose whether any of it happens. Turn the board off and MAME4droid never contacts it: NetPlay by hand works exactly as it always has.
